### C1. What is "it"?
Situation (teaching case): a typical case from the source: the string "It's not available" arrives without a screenshot or a note.
Decision: Ask the writer what "it" refers to before translating.
Why: many languages need the noun's gender or case to build the sentence, and a guess can be wrong in every language at once.
How it shows in our work: unclear lines wait in the query list instead of shipping as guesses.
Source: S2, S1

### C2. A better word than the glossary's
Situation (teaching case): a typical case from the source: the glossary keeps a feature name in English, and a local word seems to read better.
Decision: Use the glossary term and send the suggestion to the localization manager.
Why: a name policy is decided once for the whole product, not per string.
How it shows in our work: term ideas travel as proposals; the file follows the glossary.
Source: S1

### C3. Three plural forms
Situation (teaching case): a typical case from the source: the string `{count} files deleted` is going into a language with more than two plural forms.
Decision: Provide every form the language uses, with the placeholder unchanged.
Why: a single plural form is wrong grammar for most counts in such a language.
How it shows in our work: plural strings are checked against the target language's categories before hand-over.
Source: S1, S6

### C4. A pun in a slogan
Situation (teaching case): a typical case from the source: a marketing slogan depends on an English pun.
Decision: Offer two or three local versions that keep the intent, explain what each keeps and loses, and let a native reviewer choose.
Why: a literal translation keeps the words and loses the point.
How it shows in our work: lines that cannot travel literally come with options, not one silent choice.
Source: S5, S2

### C5. A privacy notice
Situation (teaching case): a typical case from the source: a privacy notice is sent for translation like any other page.
Decision: Translate it only as a reference draft and say that a qualified human translator and a legal review are needed.
Why: an error in legal meaning carries risk a draft cannot carry.
How it shows in our work: legal, medical and contractual texts are labelled reference drafts until a qualified person reviews them.
Source: S4
