# Hard questions

Outside funders check a plan against their own focus (industry, region, stage) and then look closely at the team, the market, the product, the company's governing documents and its financial statements. The questions they ask are rarely a surprise; the answers often are. This skill writes those questions down in advance, says what a good answer would need, and shows where the current deck already answers them and where it does not.

**Route:** a deck or plan and its critique (`deck-critique`) → **hard-questions** → the founder prepares answers → unproven claims go to the assumption log.

## When to use

- Before a meeting with early-stage investors, lenders or a grant panel.
- To stress-test a plan before it is written into a deck.
- Not to coach anyone to avoid questions, and not to give advice on whether to raise money, from whom or on what terms.

## Inputs

- The deck or plan, and the critique if there is one.
- Who the readers are: their usual industry, region and stage, as far as the founder knows.
- The amount and kind of money sought (a loan, a grant, a share of ownership).

## Steps

1. **Group the questions by area:** customer and problem; product and its stage; market and competitors; how customers are won and how a sale happens; team; money (use of funds, costs per customer, break-even, what the next round depends on); ownership and control; risks.
2. **Write 10 to 20 questions** in plain words, each specific enough to be answered with evidence: «Which customers make up the market figure on slide 4, and how many can the first year's sales channel reach?» rather than «Is the market big?».
3. **For each question, write what a good answer needs:** the evidence, the number and its source, the date, the document (for example signed letters of intent, a cohort table, the company's articles).
4. **Note where the deck stands now:** answered (slide), partly (what is missing), not answered.
5. **Add the ownership questions:** outside money usually means giving up part of the ownership and some control, such as a seat on the board; ask what the founders are prepared to give up, and what the money is tied to (milestones, rounds).
6. **Order the list:** the questions whose answer would change the plan most come first.
7. **Log unproven claims** in the assumption log, with how each could be checked.

## Rules

- **Questions, not verdicts.** The list never says whether the company deserves money, never puts a price on the company or its shares, and never says whether anyone should put money in.
- **No trick questions** and no questions about the founders' private lives, health, family or background.
- **No guesses presented as facts.** What a reader is «likely» to ask is said that way.
- **Money and law go to professionals:** terms, securities rules, tax and the legal documents go to a lawyer or an accountant; the financial model to the startup finance analyst.

## Checks before hand-over

- Between 10 and 20 questions, each in an area, each with what a good answer needs and the deck's status.
- The ownership and control questions are included.
- The order puts plan-changing questions first.
- No sentence rates the company or advises on putting money in.

## Artefacts

- `pitch/<deck>/hard-questions.md`: the grouped list with good-answer needs and status.

## Hand-over

- **To the founder:** the list, to prepare answers and evidence.
- **To the startup finance analyst:** questions about the model, costs and break-even.
- **To the assumption log:** every claim the questions show to be unproven.
- **To a lawyer or an accountant (people):** questions about terms, ownership documents and tax.
