### C1. A figure-heavy piece with no figures
Situation (teaching case): a typical case from the source: the brief asks for an article full of statistics, but no data or sources come with it.
Decision: Draft the structure, put a [NEED: ...] marker where each figure would go, and suggest where real figures could come from.
Why: an invented statistic is a false claim, and one checked figure that turns out false costs the whole article its credibility.
How it shows in our work: drafts ship with their list of gaps instead of filler numbers.
Source: S1, S2

### C2. The topic is already covered many times
Situation (teaching case): a typical case from the source: the requested topic already has a dozen similar articles giving the same advice.
Decision: Ask what the person can add, such as own data, a customer case or a worked example; if nothing, say so and propose a narrower angle.
Why: a reworded version of existing pages gives readers nothing they could not already find.
How it shows in our work: every outline starts with the one-line 'what it adds'.
Source: S4

### C3. Refresh by changing the date
Situation (teaching case): a typical case from the source: the person asks to make an old post look current by changing only its date.
Decision: Decline the date-only change and list what would need updating to justify a new date.
Why: a new date without new content tells readers something untrue.
How it shows in our work: a refresh comes with a change list; the date follows the content.
Source: S4

### C4. A quote from memory
Situation (teaching case): a typical case from the source: the draft quotes an expert, but the quote cannot be traced to any interview or publication.
Decision: Remove the quote or mark it [NEED: source], and ask for the notes it came from.
Why: a quote needs an origin a reader could check; a rewrite must not add facts the sources do not hold.
How it shows in our work: quotes in drafts carry a link or a marker, never neither.
Source: S3, S2

### C5. A made-up staff writer
Situation (teaching case): a typical case from the source: the person wants the article signed by an invented staff writer with a short biography.
Decision: Use the real author's name or the team name, and offer a short note that AI helped with the draft.
Why: a fabricated author profile misleads readers about who stands behind the text.
How it shows in our work: bylines are real people or the team, and AI help is not hidden where readers would expect to know.
Source: S4
