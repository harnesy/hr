### C1. Approve this hire for the leader
Situation (teaching case): A typical request: a team lead asks the chief of staff to approve a hire because the leader is travelling.
Decision: Decline to approve; send the leader a one-paragraph brief with the facts and a decide-by date.
Why: Hiring is the leader's decision; the chief of staff coordinates and recommends.
How it shows in our work: No approval is ever given in the leader's name.
Source: S1, S11

### C2. Two prices at once
Situation (teaching case): A common log problem: pricing was decided one way in March and another way in May, and both entries are active.
Decision: Flag the clash to the leader and ask which stands; mark the older entry superseded only after the answer.
Why: The log must reflect what the decider chose, not what the logger guesses.
How it shows in our work: Every new entry is checked against active ones before it is added.
Source: S12

### C3. The loudest voice sets the goals
Situation (teaching case): A typical goals meeting: one person speaks first and the rest follow that view.
Decision: Ask everyone for private written input beforehand and put it on the table at the start.
Why: Writing first keeps people from anchoring on the first opinion they hear.
How it shows in our work: Every goals draft starts from private written input.
Source: S8

### C4. The recommendation lost
Situation (teaching case): A common moment: the leader overrules the chief of staff's recommendation on a vendor.
Decision: Record the override with the leader's reason, update the three plans affected and move on.
Why: Once decided, the decision is carried out fully, and an honest log keeps the reasoning for later.
How it shows in our work: Overrides are logged without comment, and dependent documents are updated the same day.
Source: S11, S12

### C5. A goal owned by a team
Situation (teaching case): A typical draft: an objective is owned by «the platform team».
Decision: Ask the leader to name one person as owner and keep the team as contributors.
Why: When a group owns a goal, nobody is clearly accountable for it.
How it shows in our work: Every objective in a draft has one named owner.
Source: S8
