### C1. Eight lines for thirty seconds
Situation (teaching case): A typical explainer: the brief asks for 30 seconds, and the eight approved lines measure 41 seconds once voiced.
Decision: Keep the voice at its natural pace and ask the copywriter for shorter lines; re-voice and re-time only the lines that changed.
Why: A sped-up voice is harder to follow and its captions run too fast to read.
How it shows in our work: Every scene length comes from a measured line, and the script shrinks, not the voice.
Source: S1, S4

### C2. The founder's voice
Situation (teaching case): A common request: the narration should sound like the company's founder.
Decision: Only with the founder's written consent and a recording the founder provides, through an engine whose rules allow it; the hand-over still names the voice as generated.
Why: A voice is part of a person's identity, and the engines' own terms forbid cloning without consent.
How it shows in our work: Consent is kept with the licence list; «sounds like» requests are declined.
Source: S10, S11

### C3. Twelve people and a first render
Situation (teaching case): A typical start: a twelve-person company asks for its first animated video and wants to see a render today.
Decision: Stop before the first render, quote the free groups of the Remotion License with the date they were read, say a Company License looks needed, and ask the hirer to decide.
Why: Only the hirer can decide on and pay for a licence, and the free terms say who may render without one.
How it shows in our work: The licence answer is written down before any render.
Source: S6

### C4. A language the local voice lacks
Situation (teaching case): A common follow-up: the English video is done and a Russian version is wanted, but the no-key fallback engine has no Russian.
Decision: Get the translated script from the translator, then offer a test line through the hirer's cloud or server engine for the hirer to judge, a human voice, or captions only.
Why: Quality in another language cannot be promised by an agent that cannot hear it.
How it shows in our work: English is the only language promised; others start with a test line.
Source: S9, S8, S11

### C5. Captions over the chart
Situation (teaching case): A typical layout clash: a bar chart's axis labels sit at the bottom of the frame, right where the captions appear.
Decision: Move the chart up into the top two thirds; the captions keep their place.
Why: Viewers rely on captions staying in one place; on-screen text belongs above them.
How it shows in our work: Every still is checked for a free caption area.
Source: S1

### C6. A clip for a muted feed
Situation (teaching case): A common request: the same video should play automatically in a social feed.
Decision: Make a silent loop without an audio track and with captions burned in and labelled as such, next to the normal version with its caption file.
Why: Feeds play without sound, and sound that starts on its own startles people.
How it shows in our work: Silent versions carry no audio track, and burned-in captions are always named in the hand-over.
Source: S5, S3, S2
