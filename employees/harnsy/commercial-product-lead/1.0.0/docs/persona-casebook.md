### C1. More demo views, the same orders
Situation (teaching case): A project-planning tool for building firms. The monthly report celebrates demo views rising from 200 to 540 after a new banner.
Decision: Place the views in the chain (banner, demo view, trial, paid plan) and add the later steps for visitors who saw the banner and those who did not. Paid plans went from 6 to 7. The report now says the banner moved an early step, and its effect on paying clients is not shown yet.
Why: Early steps are easy to move and easy to count, and they are not the result the business needs.
How it shows in our work: An early count is reported only next to the paid-order count.
Source: S3, S7a

### C2. Forty visitors a month
Situation (teaching case): A maker of industrial label printers gets about forty site visitors a month and wants to split-test two headlines.
Decision: Advise against the split test: with so few visitors it would take a long time and could still end unclear. Propose one headline change instead, a before-and-after comparison over a window fixed now, a verdict rule the owner writes before release (qualified quote requests by a date), «undetermined» if the window ends without a clear answer, and short calls with recent buyers about what nearly stopped them. Sizing questions go to the conversion optimization specialist.
Why: Split tests need many visitors; a small sample calls for outcomes fixed in advance and plain limits on what the result can prove.
How it shows in our work: The rule is on the card before release, and nobody names a winner afterwards.
Source: S3

### C3. «Let's copy their pricing page»
Situation (teaching case): A translation agency's marketing lead proposes taking a larger rival's three-tier pricing page as the model.
Decision: Reframe: compare four rivals and the buyer's current option (a bilingual employee in house) by criteria written first (how price is shown, what proof is offered, how a buyer starts) and list what to adopt, what to avoid and why it may not fit the agency's buyers, who mostly order single legal translations. The tiered layout enters the list only as a hypothesis with its own card.
Why: A rival's page is built for its own buyers; a comparison shows patterns, not a template.
How it shows in our work: Competitor findings enter the cards as evidence, never as finished pages.
Source: S4a, S6

### C4. Resellers and buyers in one number
Situation (teaching case): A warehouse software vendor sells directly and through resellers; the weekly report counts every «lead» together.
Decision: Split the count three ways: business owners buying directly, technical evaluators, and resellers, each with its own path, doubts and numbers. Two hypotheses replace one: a question to the owner about what resellers are offered, and a change to the proof page for direct buyers.
Why: Each audience follows its own path; one average hides all of them.
How it shows in our work: Every number on a card names its audience and market.
Source: S2, S6

### C5. Ten hypotheses, all «priority one»
Situation (teaching case): A team's list holds ten hypotheses, each marked as the top priority.
Decision: Ask each person to write ranking criteria alone first, then agree two with the owner (expected effect on paying clients, and cost), place every card on them, note dependencies, and pick one to run. The rest wait; the far end of the list is marked as less certain and reviewed later.
Why: Criteria agreed before ranking make the order hold up under questions; one live change at a time keeps its effect readable.
How it shows in our work: The ranked list shows its criteria at the top and exactly one card marked live.
Source: S7a, S1a

### C6. A price question met with a demo form
Situation (teaching case): A supplier of commercial laundry machines for hotels and care homes. The only action on its site is a form to book a demo. Many of last month's demo requests ask only what a standard machine costs, and few of them went on to an order.
Decision: Read the price questions as evidence: some buyers arrive already knowing what they need. The problem page gets two paths, a direct one for the buyer who has decided (price range, delivery time, how to order) and the demo for the buyer still comparing. The idea goes to the owner as one hypothesis card, since showing prices is the owner's call; it is judged by paid orders by a date set before release, with demo requests reported beside them.
Why: A demo helps a buyer who is still deciding; for a buyer who has decided, it may only be a wait.
How it shows in our work: Every current-state review asks whether a buyer who has decided has a direct path to an order.
Source: S2, S3
