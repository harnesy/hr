# Commercial problem

Requests usually arrive as answers: a new page, a demo button, a shorter form. Before anyone builds one, this skill writes down what the answer is meant to fix, for which buyer, and how a paying client would show that it worked. The page it produces is the reference the team returns to when a discussion drifts, and it changes when evidence arrives.

**Route:** a request or a goal → **commercial-problem** → `proposal-review` for the team's ideas against this page → `hypothesis-backlog` once the page is agreed.

## When to use

- A new request from the owner or a team: «we need more leads», «add a trial option», «redo the home page».
- A new goal or a new market, or a plan that lists activities without a commercial outcome.
- When the team argues about what the work is for.
- Not for writing a product specification or user stories (the product manager and the product owner do that), and not for choosing channels or a budget (the marketing strategist does).

## Inputs

- The request as written, who asked, and by when.
- What the business sells, to whom, in which price range, and through which route (self-serve, a sales call, a partner).
- What a buyer can actually purchase now, taken from the business's own sales papers (its prices, the offer, the contract terms it shares with you), not only from the site.
- The numbers that exist already: visitors, requests, deals, paid orders, by source and by month, each with its date. The revenue operations specialist or the product analyst can export them; you only read them.
- Notes from earlier conversations with buyers and with the team.

## Steps

1. **Goal in one line.** New paying clients or their revenue, how many or how much, by which date, and who decides. If the owner has not set it, write the question down and stop here.
2. **Turn the request back into a problem.** For a ready-made answer, ask what it should fix and for whom; write the problem without naming any solution. Mark anything that is a symptom, or a solution posing as the cause. Note where the cause may sit, without choosing yet: how the offer is positioned, how its worth is explained, trust at the moment a buyer decides, how the site is organised, the offer itself, or who the visitors are.
3. **Audiences, one by one.** For example the technical buyer who evaluates, the business owner who pays, the partner who resells. For each: the job they need done, the doubts that hold them back, the evidence they trust, and how they buy. Start short conversations with a few buyers of each type now, while this research runs; they are not saved for after the hypotheses are chosen.
4. **Markets, one by one.** A country, a company size or a sector, each with its own demand, rivals and price expectations. Two markets never share one number.
5. **Current state, read as each buyer.** Start with the visits: how many arrive, from which sources and markets, and how many come to buy rather than to read or learn. When visits are close to none, bringing buyers to the site is a problem of its own: the page keeps two lines of hypotheses apart, one on whether the site can sell to the buyers who do reach it, one on whether buyers reach it at all. Then walk the site and the sales route as each audience would: what is offered and to whom, the problem it addresses, the reasons a buyer has to believe it, the path to an order, where buyers leave, and what stays unclear. Ask two questions on the path: can a buyer who has decided go straight to an order, and is there a lighter step (a demo, a trial, a pilot) for a buyer still deciding? Record the starting numbers with their dates; without a starting point no later change can be shown to help.
6. **Rivals.** Before looking, write the comparison criteria: for each rival, how it shows what it is worth, earns trust, meets doubts and moves a buyer to an order. Include the buyer's current way of doing the job, such as doing nothing or doing it in house. For each rival, note why its approach may succeed, its strong and weak points, what suits our buyers and what to avoid. A rival selling well is no proof that its wording is the reason. Never copy a rival's page, wording or offer. Deep or dated research goes to the competitive analyst, with these criteria.
7. **Market questions.** Is there demand, how many buyers, where, how crowded, and what they pay today for alternatives. Mark which answers come from existing sources and which need direct research (calls, short surveys) run by the people who own those conversations.
8. **Missing questions.** Add what the request did not ask: who is not ready to buy and why, what would make a ready buyer act now, which proof is missing, when a trial or a demo really helps a buyer decide, and whether the page argues value in step with how the buyer pays (a single purchase or a recurring payment).
9. **Out of scope.** Write what this problem does not cover, so nobody solves it by accident.
10. **Facts and assumptions.** Mark every statement as fact (with source and date) or as assumption (with how it could be checked).
11. **Decisions for the owner.** List apart what only the owner can decide: the target, the market to start with, a change of price or offer, what stays out.

## Rules

- No solution on the page. A change idea goes to `proposal-review` or onto a hypothesis card, never into the problem statement.
- Each audience and each market keeps its own line; an average is shown only next to the split.
- No market figure without a named, dated published source; an unsourced figure is an assumption.
- Conversations with buyers follow the person: one at a time, open questions, room for silence, and «why is that?» more than once. You may draft the guide; a person holds the conversation.
- Read-only: the site, the analytics and the CRM are read, never changed.
- The page lives: when the owner recasts the goal or fresh evidence comes in, revise it that day and list the work in progress the change affects; that work waits until it is checked against the revised page.

## Checks before hand-over

- The goal names paying clients or revenue, a number and a date, or says plainly that the owner has not set them.
- No sentence prescribes a solution.
- Every audience and every market has its own path, doubts and evidence.
- Starting numbers carry dates and sources.
- Visits are read by volume, intent and market; when they are close to none, the two lines of hypotheses are kept apart.
- The page says whether a buyer who has decided has a direct path to an order, and whether a buyer still deciding has a lighter step.
- Rivals and the buyer's current way are compared by criteria written before looking, with what to adopt and what to avoid; nothing is copied.
- Buyer conversations have started during the research, or the page says why not yet.
- Every statement is marked fact or assumption.
- Out of scope and the owner's decisions are filled in.
- After a recast goal or fresh evidence, the page shows the date of the revision and the work in progress it holds back.

## Artefacts

- `commercial/problem-<date>.md`: one page with the goal, audiences, markets, what can be bought today, the current state per buyer (with the visits and, when they are close to none, the two lines), the rival comparison with its criteria, market questions, missing questions, out of scope, the facts-and-assumptions table, the decisions for the owner and a dated log of revisions.

## Hand-over

- **To the owner:** the page and the decisions list.
- **To the owner and the team, on the day the page changes:** what changed and which work in progress waits.
- **To the product manager:** the page, as input for the roadmap.
- **To the competitive analyst:** the questions about rivals, with the comparison criteria written in advance.
- **To `hypothesis-backlog`:** the agreed page.
