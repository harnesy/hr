# Commercial problem

Requests usually arrive as answers: a new page, a demo button, a shorter form. Before anyone builds one, this skill writes down what the answer is meant to fix, for which buyer, and how a paying client would show that it worked. The page it produces is the reference the team returns to when a discussion drifts, and it changes when evidence arrives.

**Route:** a request or a goal → **commercial-problem** → `proposal-review` for the team's ideas against this page → `hypothesis-backlog` once the page is agreed.

## When to use

- A new request from the owner or a team: «we need more leads», «add a trial option», «redo the home page».
- A new goal or a new market, or a plan that lists activities without a commercial outcome.
- When the team argues about what the work is for.
- Not for writing a product specification or user stories (the product manager and the product owner do that), and not for choosing channels or a budget (the marketing strategist does).

## Inputs

- The request as written, who asked, and by when.
- What the business sells, to whom, in which price range, and through which route (self-serve, a sales call, a partner).
- What a buyer can actually purchase now, taken from the business's own sales papers (its prices, the offer, the contract terms it shares with you), not only from the site.
- The numbers that exist already: visitors, requests, deals, paid orders, by source and by month, each with its date. The revenue operations specialist or the product analyst can export them; you only read them.
- Notes from earlier conversations with buyers and with the team.

## Steps

1. **Goal in one line.** New paying clients or their revenue, how many or how much, by which date, and who decides. If the owner has not set it, write the question down and stop here.
2. **Turn the request back into a problem.** For a ready-made answer, ask what it should fix and for whom; write the problem without naming any solution. Mark anything that is a symptom, or a solution posing as the cause. Note where the cause may sit, without choosing yet: who the visitors are, which segment the positioning speaks to, why a buyer would buy at all, the offer itself, how its worth is explained, how the site is organised, or the evidence a buyer needs before a sales conversation. Name trust as a cause only with evidence for it, such as buyers' own words or lost deals with a stated reason. Legal duties (a privacy notice, data-processing terms) are done because they are owed, on their own merits, and are not put down as the reason sales are lost. The places a business buyer looks for trust (shown competence, product quality, a demonstration, certificates, technical documents, partner referrals, pilot results) are a list of places to check, not a list of faults to assume.
3. **Segments and audiences.** Segment buyers by need, maturity in the field, budget, who owns the problem, readiness to act on what they learn, the setup they already run, and how they purchase; company size or headcount is not a segment. A message aimed at one size of buyer can push other sizes away without winning the first, so keep the positioning apart from the offers and arguments for each segment. Then the audiences, one by one: for example the technical buyer who evaluates, the business owner who pays, the partner who resells. For each: the job they need done, the doubts that hold them back, the evidence they trust, and how they buy. Start short conversations with a few buyers of each type now, while this research runs; they are not saved for after the hypotheses are chosen.
4. **Buying motive.** Before judging how the site explains the product, write down why a buyer would buy at all: the problem that sends them looking, what happens if it stays unsolved, how they handle it today and what that costs, why that is no longer good enough, who inside wants the change, who decides and from which budget, which real alternatives they weigh (an own team, a contractor, tools already in place, doing nothing), and what makes them act now rather than later. A clear description of the product is not a reason to buy. Gaps here become questions for the buyer conversations, not guesses.
5. **Markets, one by one.** A country, a region or a sector, each with its own demand, rivals and price expectations. Two markets never share one number.
6. **Current state, read as each buyer.** Start with the visits: how many arrive, from which sources and markets, and how many come to buy rather than to read or learn. When visits are close to none, bringing buyers to the site is a problem of its own: the page keeps two lines of hypotheses apart, one on whether the site can sell to the buyers who do reach it, one on whether buyers reach it at all. Then walk the site and the sales route as each audience would: what is offered and to whom, the problem it addresses, the reasons a buyer has to believe it, the path to an order, where buyers leave, and what stays unclear. Read the path in the business's own sales model. Where sales run through a conversation (a request, then a call, a proposal, an agreement and an invoice), a request form is a real way to buy; a missing public price, self-serve checkout or online payment is not a fault unless evidence shows buyers want one. Keep three problems apart, each with its own numbers and causes: buyers who do not find how to begin; buyers who find it and still write nothing; requests after which no paid order follows. List the intents buyers arrive with (order a check, see a demo, get a proposal or a price, talk to an expert, run a pilot, buy) and the next step each one finds: none is the default, each costs something and draws different people, and they are not merged into one assumed chain. Check that a buyer who has made up their mind reaches a sales conversation without a required sign-up, demo or pilot first. The path on the site is one part of the sales process; the rest is read from the sales records. Record the starting numbers with their dates; without a starting point no later change can be shown to help.
7. **Rivals.** Before looking, write the comparison criteria, about offers rather than parts of a site: what each rival offers, to which buyers, for which problem, against which alternatives, and why a buyer would choose it. Include the buyer's current way of doing the job, such as doing nothing or doing it in house. For each rival, note why its approach may succeed, its strong and weak points, what suits our buyers and what to avoid. A rival's practice (a public price, a free check, self sign-up) shows that it exists, not what it earns: how many of its visitors buy, what a client costs it to win, its deal size and how much its site does in its sales are not known. Prices are compared only together with what the buyer gets for them, and pricing is decided on its own, not taken from a rival's site. Never copy a rival's page, wording or offer. Deep or dated research goes to the competitive analyst, with these criteria.
8. **Market questions.** Is there demand, how many buyers, where, how crowded, and what they pay today for alternatives. Mark which answers come from existing sources and which need direct research (calls, short surveys) run by the people who own those conversations.
9. **Missing questions.** Add what the request did not ask: who is not ready to buy and why, what would make a ready buyer act now, which proof is missing, when a trial or a demo really helps a buyer decide, and whether the page argues value in step with how the buyer pays (a single purchase or a recurring payment).
10. **Out of scope.** Write what this problem does not cover, so nobody solves it by accident.
11. **Facts and assumptions, layer by layer.** Mark every statement by its layer: an observation (what is there, with source and date), an interpretation (what it may mean), a claim about cause (that it changes what buyers do), or a forecast effect (what a change would bring). Each layer needs its own evidence; a statement without it is an assumption, with how it could be checked. «The form has nine fields» is an observation; that buyers find it tiring, that this costs requests, and that a shorter form would bring paying clients are three further claims.
12. **Decisions for the owner.** List apart what only the owner can decide: the target, the market to start with, a change of price or offer, what stays out.

## Rules

- No solution on the page. A change idea goes to `proposal-review` or onto a hypothesis card, never into the problem statement.
- Each audience and each market keeps its own line; an average is shown only next to the split.
- No market figure without a named, dated published source; an unsourced figure is an assumption.
- No default findings: a missing public price, self-serve checkout, legal page or trust mark is written down as an observation and becomes a cause only with evidence. A price or a notice that the law requires a seller to show (for example when selling to consumers) is a duty, not a hypothesis: it is shown whatever the sales model.
- Conversations with buyers follow the person: one at a time, open questions, room for silence, and «why is that?» more than once. You may draft the guide; a person holds the conversation.
- Read-only: the site, the analytics and the CRM are read, never changed.
- The page lives: when the owner recasts the goal or fresh evidence comes in, revise it that day and list the work in progress the change affects; that work waits until it is checked against the revised page.

## Checks before hand-over

- The goal names paying clients or revenue, a number and a date, or says plainly that the owner has not set them.
- No sentence prescribes a solution.
- Segments are drawn by need, maturity, budget, problem owner and way of purchasing, not by company size; every audience and every market has its own path, doubts and evidence.
- The buying motive is written down, with its gaps marked as questions for buyers.
- Starting numbers carry dates and sources.
- Visits are read by volume, intent and market; when they are close to none, the two lines of hypotheses are kept apart.
- The path is read in the business's own sales model; a request form is not marked as a fault for being a form, and nothing missing (a price, a checkout, a legal page) is listed as a cause without evidence.
- The three problems (no way found to begin, nothing written, request never paid) are kept apart, each with its numbers.
- Each buyer intent has its own next step, none is the default, and a buyer who has made up their mind meets no required sign-up, demo or pilot.
- Trust is named as a cause only with evidence.
- Rivals and the buyer's current way are compared by offers, buyers and alternatives, with criteria written before looking and what to adopt and what to avoid; nothing is copied.
- Buyer conversations have started during the research, or the page says why not yet.
- Every statement carries its layer (observation, interpretation, claim about cause, forecast effect); one without evidence is marked as an assumption.
- Out of scope and the owner's decisions are filled in.
- After a recast goal or fresh evidence, the page shows the date of the revision and the work in progress it holds back.

## Artefacts

- `commercial/problem-<date>.md`: one page with the goal, segments, audiences, the buying motive, markets, what can be bought today, the current state per buyer in the business's own sales model (with the visits and, when they are close to none, the two lines; the three path problems; the next step per intent), the rival comparison with its criteria, market questions, missing questions, out of scope, the table of statements by layer, the decisions for the owner and a dated log of revisions.

## Hand-over

- **To the owner:** the page and the decisions list.
- **To the owner and the team, on the day the page changes:** what changed and which work in progress waits.
- **To the product manager:** the page, as input for the roadmap.
- **To the competitive analyst:** the questions about rivals, with the comparison criteria written in advance.
- **To `hypothesis-backlog`:** the agreed page.
