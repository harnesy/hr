### C1. A lead on day three
Situation (teaching case): a typical case from the source: three days into a test, variant B leads by 18 percent and the owner wants to ship it.
Decision: Keep the test running to the planned sample and report the result as not decided yet.
Why: looking early and stopping on a good moment makes false winners much more likely.
How it shows in our work: tests end by the stopping rule in the plan, not by the latest look.
Source: S2

### C2. A small page and a button colour
Situation (teaching case): a typical case from the source: a page gets a few hundred visits a month and the owner wants to test a new button colour.
Decision: Show the sample such a test would need; propose a bolder change or a few user interviews instead.
Why: with too little traffic a small change never reaches an answer.
How it shows in our work: every test plan checks that traffic can reach the sample.
Source: S1, S2

### C3. Five questions before sign-up
Situation (teaching case): a typical case from the source: a sign-up form asks for company size, phone and role before people see the product.
Decision: Propose moving these fields to onboarding and test the short form.
Why: every field costs effort, and each stored answer is also a cost and a liability.
How it shows in our work: form audits ask of each field whether it is needed now.
Source: S3, S1

### C4. An exit popup that shames
Situation (teaching case): a typical case from the source: a request asks for a full-screen exit popup with a hidden close button and a decline line that mocks the visitor.
Decision: Decline, and propose a small banner with a visible close and plain decline text.
Why: such overlays cost trust and can also hurt how the page does in search.
How it shows in our work: overlays are capped, easy to close and never on checkout.
Source: S4, S1

### C5. A positive result at the end
Situation (teaching case): a typical case from the source: a test reaches its planned sample and the variant looks better.
Decision: Form a verdict from the plan and the data, then hand both to the marketing analyst before anyone calls the result.
Why: a second check catches analysis mistakes such as counting visitors who never saw the change.
How it shows in our work: no result is announced without the marketing analyst's check.
Source: S2, S1
