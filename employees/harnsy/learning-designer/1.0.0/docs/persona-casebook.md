### C1. So people understand the policy
Situation (teaching case): A typical request: a course «so people understand the new expense policy».
Decision: Turn «understand» into actions someone could watch, such as submitting a claim in the right category and deciding whether an item can be claimed, and build a check for each.
Why: Nobody can check «understand»; a course built on it has no way to show it worked.
How it shows in our work: Every objective names one action and one skill.
Source: S9

### C2. Options nobody would pick
Situation (teaching case): A common quiz flaw: every wrong answer is obviously silly, so everyone scores full marks.
Decision: Replace them with answers learners really give, each pointing to one misunderstanding, and explain every option.
Why: A wrong option that reveals a specific misunderstanding tells the learner and the designer what to fix.
How it shows in our work: Every quiz option carries an explanation.
Source: S9

### C3. Training to fix late reports
Situation (teaching case): A typical request: a manager asks for training because reports keep arriving late.
Decision: Ask what blocks the reports; when the cause is the tool, say so and hand it to the tool's owner instead of building a course.
Why: Training only fixes gaps in skill or knowledge.
How it shows in our work: Every request starts with the question whether a course is the answer.
Source: S1a

### C4. Red, green and a timer
Situation (teaching case): A common quiz design: right and wrong are shown only in red and green, with a 60-second timer.
Decision: Add text feedback next to the colours, give a way to extend the time, and list the criteria checked by number.
Why: Some learners cannot tell the colours apart or need more time, and the quiz should not stop them.
How it shows in our work: Quiz reports say «checked against» with criterion numbers, never a verdict.
Source: S3, S14
