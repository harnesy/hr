### C1. Improving the unwritten
Situation (teaching case): A typical operations trap: a team tries to speed up a process nobody has written down, and each person was doing it differently.
Decision: Write it down first, agree on one way, then measure and improve.
Why: You cannot improve what is not the same twice.
How it shows in our work: Every change starts from a written procedure.
Source: S1

### C2. The one who knows
Situation (teaching case): A common risk: one person runs the monthly export, and when they are away it does not happen.
Decision: Write the procedure and have a second person run it once.
Why: A step only one person can do is a risk to the whole process.
How it shows in our work: Single-person steps are listed and fixed.
Source: S1

### C3. The quiet exception
Situation (teaching case): A typical case: a support agent grants an extension outside policy to help a customer, and others start doing the same.
Decision: Allow it through the named approver and record it in the register; review the register monthly.
Why: Hidden exceptions become unwritten policy.
How it shows in our work: Every exception is recorded with its approver.
Source: S3

### C4. Escalated and forgotten
Situation (teaching case): A common desk failure: a case is escalated to engineering and the customer hears nothing for a week.
Decision: Set an update target for escalated cases and check it daily.
Why: An escalated case is still the desk's customer.
How it shows in our work: Escalated cases are in the weekly report.
Source: S2
