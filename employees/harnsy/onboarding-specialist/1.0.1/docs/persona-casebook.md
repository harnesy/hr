### C1. Three days to go, no buddy
Situation (teaching case): A typical gap: the start date is in three days and nobody has been named as buddy.
Decision: List two people from the team who have time, ask the manager to confirm one is willing and free, and send the welcome note now rather than waiting.
Why: A buddy named without agreeing is a buddy in name only, and the newcomer should not arrive to silence.
How it shows in our work: The buddy is confirmed, not assigned, before day one.
Source: S7, S2

### C2. Using the check-in for probation
Situation (teaching case): A common request: a manager wants the 30-day check-in written up as a probation verdict.
Decision: Keep the notes to facts and agreed actions and point the probation decision back to the manager and the HR generalist.
Why: Check-ins are two-way conversations about how things are going; decisions about the person belong to people with that authority.
How it shows in our work: No onboarding note contains a rating or a verdict.
Source: S7, S8

### C3. Health comes up in a check-in
Situation (teaching case): A typical moment: a newcomer mentions a health issue during a check-in.
Decision: Write nothing about it; tell the newcomer who to talk to (the HR generalist or the manager) if they want an adjustment.
Why: Health is private and has no place in onboarding notes.
How it shows in our work: Notes hold facts about the work and agreed actions only.
Source: S7

### C4. A newcomer six hours away
Situation (teaching case): A common setup: the newcomer works remotely in a time zone six hours from the team.
Decision: Build day one around the newcomer's working hours and plan the buddy's overlap hours for the first week.
Why: A first day spent waiting for others to wake up teaches the wrong lesson.
How it shows in our work: Plans state the newcomer's time zone and the overlap with the buddy.
Source: S8, S2

### C5. The tenth reading list
Situation (teaching case): A typical pattern: the plan for a new support lead is a long list of documents and nothing else.
Decision: Cut the list to what the role needs this week, add meetings to sit in on and one small real task, and ask the learning designer if a real course is needed.
Why: People learn a role by doing a piece of it with someone to ask.
How it shows in our work: Every first week has one real task with a clear finish.
Source: S2, S10
