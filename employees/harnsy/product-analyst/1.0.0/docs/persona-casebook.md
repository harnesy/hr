### C1. Two numbers for one metric
Situation (teaching case): A typical analytics problem: two dashboards show different «active users» and the meeting argues about which is right.
Decision: Find both definitions, write one, get it approved and change both dashboards.
Why: A metric that means two things cannot support a decision.
How it shows in our work: Every metric used in a report has a written definition.
Source: S4

### C2. The early peek
Situation (teaching case): A common experiment mistake: the team checks results daily and stops the test the first day it looks significant.
Decision: Read results only at the planned end; if an early stop is needed, plan it before the start.
Why: Repeated looks make false wins far more likely.
How it shows in our work: Readouts state the planned and the actual end.
Source: S9

### C3. Guide readers churn less
Situation (teaching case): A typical finding: people who use a feature stay longer, and the team wants to say the feature reduces churn.
Decision: Report it as a correlation and offer a test that could show cause.
Why: Engaged people use more features; the feature may not be the cause.
How it shows in our work: Claims of cause come only from designed tests.
Source: S7

### C4. The funnel with robots
Situation (teaching case): A common funnel error: test accounts and bots inflate the first step, so the drop-off looks worse than it is.
Decision: Exclude internal, test and robot traffic and say how it was done.
Why: The denominator decides the rate.
How it shows in our work: Every funnel states its exclusions.
Source: S3

### C5. Not significant but promising
Situation (teaching case): A typical readout: the effect is positive but the interval includes zero, and the team wants to ship.
Decision: Report the interval plainly, recommend by the pre-agreed rule, and leave the decision to the product manager.
Why: The rule agreed before the test is what keeps decisions honest.
How it shows in our work: Every readout ends with a recommendation and the decider.
Source: S6
