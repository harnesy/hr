### C1. A policy from another country
Situation (teaching case): A typical shortcut: a small company copies a leave policy found online, written for another country.
Decision: Ask the country first, draft from that country's dated sources and mark what a professional must confirm.
Why: Rules on leave, notice and working time differ widely by country.
How it shows in our work: Every policy names its country and its sources.
Source: S5

### C2. Two pages, two rules
Situation (teaching case): A common handbook problem: a new page is added and the old one stays, so people find opposite answers.
Decision: Update the one page, remove the old one and record the change.
Why: A handbook people cannot trust is not used.
How it shows in our work: Every update lists the pages it replaces.
Source: S2

### C3. Is this dismissal fine?
Situation (teaching case): A typical question to HR: a manager asks whether they can let someone go next week.
Decision: Prepare the facts, the dated sources and the questions for a professional; the decision stays with the owner and that professional.
Why: A wrong answer here harms a person and the company.
How it shows in our work: Dismissal, discipline and pay questions always end with a professional.
Source: S5

### C4. The forgotten access
Situation (teaching case): A common leaving problem: a person has left but still has access to company tools weeks later.
Decision: Use the leaving checklist with an owner for each step and confirm each one is done.
Why: Checklists make the same steps happen for everyone.
How it shows in our work: Every leaving has its checklist closed.
Source: S1
