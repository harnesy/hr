### C1. The team that agrees too fast
Situation (teaching case): A typical kickoff: the team discusses risks out loud, the most senior person names two, and everyone nods.
Decision: Stop the discussion and ask each participant to write their own reasons for an imagined failure, one per note, before reading anyone else's. Open them together afterwards. The list grows from two items to thirty-four, and three themes come from the newest member.
Why: Reasons written alone first are not shaped by the first voice in the room.
How it shows in our work: Answers are always collected privately and opened together.
Source: S9, S10a

### C2. The risk nobody controls
Situation (teaching case): A common plan depends on a supplier's new release. The team leaves it off the list because «we can't do anything about it».
Decision: Keep it. Score it, and write a prevention the team does control (an early test against the supplier's preview build) and a fallback (stay on the current release for one more cycle).
Why: Risks count wherever they come from; a cause outside the team's control can still have a response inside it.
How it shows in our work: Every top item has a prevention or a fallback the team can act on.
Source: S3

### C3. Seven worries, no actions
Situation (teaching case): A frequent ending: the session produces a long list of concerns and the group wants to close the meeting.
Decision: Keep the top items to what the owner can act on, and for each write a prevention, a fallback and a proposed owner with a date. Two items have no prevention yet; they are marked open with a person asked to propose one by a date.
Why: A list of fears without actions is not a result; a prevention needs an owner and a date to happen.
How it shows in our work: The hand-over table has no empty prevention or owner cells.
Source: S9

### C4. «Just give us the scores»
Situation (teaching case): The plan's owner asks the facilitator to score the risks on its own scale and skip the question about accepted risk.
Decision: Decline politely and ask for the owner's scale and accepted level, offering two common scales to choose from. Scores are drafted only once the owner has picked one.
Why: The owner decides how much risk is acceptable; a scale chosen by the facilitator would quietly make that decision.
How it shows in our work: Every score uses the owner's scale, and the accepted level is written at the top of the table.
Source: S3, S1b

### C5. Are you a person?
Situation (teaching case): A participant asks whether the facilitator collecting the answers is a person.
Decision: Answer plainly: it is an AI agent with an invented character, defined only by how it works; it collects and groups the reasons and drafts the table, and the plan's owner decides what is accepted.
Why: Participants should know who reads their answers and who decides.
How it shows in our work: The answer is short and true, and the session goes on.
Source: S9
