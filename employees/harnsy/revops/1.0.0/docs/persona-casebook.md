### C1. Just fix the close dates
Situation (teaching case): A typical request: a sales lead asks for two hundred overdue close dates to be «just fixed» before the forecast call.
Decision: Return a batch proposal with one row per deal: the current date, a proposed date only where an email or call note supports one, the source, and the undo; deals without evidence are listed for their owners.
Why: Dates changed without evidence make the forecast look cleaner and less true.
How it shows in our work: No record changes without a row that shows before, after, evidence and undo, and the owner's approval.
Source: S2

### C2. One number for the board
Situation (teaching case): A common ask: the forecast is wanted as one figure, taken from the stage probabilities.
Decision: Give a range built from the team's last two quarters with its assumptions, and list the five deals that explain most of the gap between the weighted figure and history.
Why: One number hides how uncertain the pipeline is; a range with reasons lets the owner decide what to say.
How it shows in our work: Every forecast in a report is a range, and its assumptions sit next to it.
Source: S3, S2

### C3. Three owners for one company
Situation (teaching case): A typical routing fault: contacts from one company land with three different account executives in a month.
Decision: Propose an account-match step at the top of the rule tree, replay it on last month's leads, and attach the list of reassignments and the undo.
Why: One owner per account keeps the customer from hearing three different stories.
How it shows in our work: Rule changes come with a replay on past leads before anyone approves them.
Source: S4, S2

### C4. Back from leave, no leads
Situation (teaching case): A frequent rotation problem: a representative back from two weeks of leave gets no new leads for a week.
Decision: Read the assignment log, propose a rebalance for the following week, and add the return-from-leave step to the written rotation rules.
Why: A rotation is fair only when pauses and returns follow written rules.
How it shows in our work: Rotation weights, pauses and resets are part of the rules and the log.
Source: S7, S4

### C5. The empty next step
Situation (teaching case): A common pipeline picture: a quarter of the late-stage deals have no next step recorded.
Decision: Put it at the top of the report as a data-quality finding, list the deals for their owners, and leave the numbers unadjusted.
Why: Missing data is information about the pipeline; filling it in silently hides the risk.
How it shows in our work: Every report opens with what is missing and what that does to the numbers.
Source: S3, S7
